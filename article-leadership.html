Leadership Culture and the Cost of Weak Public Norms

Kunle J. Adeboye · Apr 25, 2026 · 8 min read
The way leaders behave — particularly when they believe no one is watching — defines the culture of any institution far more than its formal rules.

Leadership culture is invisible until it fails. It is the set of unwritten norms, expectations, and behaviours that determine what is acceptable in positions of power — what leaders do, what they tolerate, and what they model for those who follow them. In Nigeria, weak public norms around leadership have become one of the most significant barriers to governance reform.

What Leadership Culture Means in Practice

Formal rules rarely determine how organizations actually behave. What matters more is the informal culture — the examples set by those in senior positions, the behaviours that are rewarded or punished, and the narratives that leaders tell about their own roles and responsibilities.

When leaders routinely use public resources for private purposes, normalize opacity, and escape accountability without consequence, they do not merely harm governance directly. They set the cultural benchmark for everyone beneath them in the hierarchy.

The Normalization of Poor Leadership

One of the most damaging effects of weak leadership norms is normalization. When poor governance becomes the expected standard — when citizens are surprised by integrity rather than by corruption — a society has entered a deeply problematic equilibrium.

“Leadership culture sets the ceiling for what an institution can achieve and the floor for what citizens will accept.”

Changing the Culture

Cultural change in public institutions requires more than rhetoric. It demands consistent enforcement: leaders who are held accountable set a different example than leaders who are merely criticized. It requires public recognition of integrity: naming and rewarding good public leadership, not just exposing bad leadership. And it requires patience: institutional culture changes slowly, through sustained pressure and visible consequences.

Nigeria has extraordinary talent in its public institutions. What it often lacks is the leadership culture that would allow that talent to thrive rather than be smothered by the norms around it.
