Why Institutions Matter More Than Individual Leaders

Kunle J. Adeboye · May 9, 2026 · 7 min read
Strong institutions shape how power is used, how decisions are made, and whether public trust can survive political change.

Nigeria’s governance challenges are often discussed through the actions of individual leaders. But leadership alone cannot explain why public systems succeed, fail, endure, or break down. Institutions matter because they shape incentives, define responsibility, and determine whether public authority serves the common good or private interest.

What Institutions Actually Do

An institution is not a building or a government ministry. It is a set of rules, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that determine how people behave — especially when no one is watching. Strong institutions make good behaviour the rational choice, even for self-interested actors.

This is why nations with strong institutional frameworks tend to perform consistently across different administrations, while nations dependent on individual leaders experience wild swings in performance tied to whoever holds power.

The Nigeria Pattern

Nigeria exhibits a classic pattern of personality-dependent governance. Each new administration brings hope, new appointments, and policy announcements. But without institutional continuity — merit-based civil service, independent regulators, transparent procurement — the machinery of state cannot sustain progress across leadership transitions.

“Institutions outlast individuals. Rules and incentives shape political behaviour far more than character alone.”

Comparative Evidence

Botswana, once one of Africa’s poorest nations, built independent institutions around its diamond wealth in the 1970s. Today it ranks among Africa’s most stable democracies with some of the continent’s lowest corruption levels. The difference was not the quality of individual leaders. It was the institutional framework those leaders built and respected.

The Path Forward

Nigeria does not need to wait for perfect leaders. It needs to build institutions strong enough that imperfect leaders are constrained, accountable, and replaceable. This means strengthening the independence of regulatory bodies, the civil service commission, the judiciary, and anti-corruption agencies — not just appointing better individuals into broken systems.

Public trust depends on whether systems are fair, reliable, and accountable — not on whether the person at the top happens to be honest.
