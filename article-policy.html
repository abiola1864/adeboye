Public Policy in Practice: Why Implementation Is Where Reform Succeeds or Fails

Kunle J. Adeboye · Apr 18, 2026 · 7 min read
Nigeria produces ambitious policy documents. What it struggles to produce is sustained, consistent implementation. The gap between the two is where reform goes to die.

Nigeria is not short of policy ambition. Vision 2020, the Economic Recovery and Growth Plan, the Nigeria Agenda 2050 — the country has produced detailed frameworks for transformation across every sector of public life. What it has consistently struggled to do is implement them. Implementation is not a technical afterthought. It is where the real work of governance happens, and where most reform efforts in Nigeria have stalled.

Why Implementation Fails

Implementation failure is rarely accidental. It follows a recognizable pattern: a policy is designed at the top with insufficient input from those who must execute it; the institutional capacity to deliver is assumed rather than built; monitoring systems are weak or absent; and political commitment evaporates when the costs of change become visible.

Each of these failures is addressable. None of them is inevitable. But they require deliberate attention — not just to what government wants to achieve, but to how the machinery of state will actually deliver it.

The Capacity Question

Implementation requires capacity: trained personnel, adequate funding, clear lines of accountability, and the information systems needed to track performance. Nigeria’s ministries and agencies often lack one or more of these elements.

“A policy without an implementation plan is a wish. A plan without capacity is a disappointment. Capacity without accountability is waste.”

What Works

The evidence from countries that have successfully implemented difficult reforms points to several consistent factors: political leadership that sustains commitment beyond the announcement phase; clear assignment of responsibility to specific institutions and individuals; regular public reporting on progress; and feedback mechanisms that allow course correction when things go wrong.

None of these is beyond Nigeria’s reach. The challenge is not capability. It is prioritization, political will, and the institutional discipline to follow through.
